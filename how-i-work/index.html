A repeatable way to find what’s not working, and fix it

The method, documentation and habits I’ve used to build testing and optimization programs at Ancestry, AWS and Elastic, and how I lead the people who run them.

The loop

  1. Set goals and tracking

    Tie every test to signups, leads or revenue, and confirm it’s measured before launch.

  2. Find the opportunities

    Analytics, heatmaps, session recordings, surveys and usability research, read side by side.

  3. Write the hypothesis

    If we change X, we expect Y, because of Z. Evidence, not preference.

  4. Scope the sample and timing

    Estimate visitors and duration up front so the data is neither under- nor oversampled.

  5. Build and QA

    Across devices and browsers, with no other launches on the page mid-test.

  6. Analyze the impact

    Significance on primary and secondary metrics, then lifetime value to size the annual gain.

  7. Ship, iterate and share

    Winners become the default, losers stop at the planned sample, and every result is archived and shared.

Deciding what to test

Plenty of data surfaces opportunities, but not every finding needs a test. Each one gets the right next step:

Just do it
An obvious fix that doesn’t need a test.
Instrument
A tracking or reporting gap to close first.
Investigate
The cause isn’t clear yet; dig deeper before writing a hypothesis.
Test
A clear opportunity to test and optimize.

Test ideas are then scored and ranked into a roadmap:

RICE = Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort

Each factor is scored 1 to 5 against defined scales, so a stakeholder’s favorite idea and a data-backed one are compared on the same terms.

Documenting every test

Consistent documentation is what turns individual tests into a program people can learn from. Every test I run uses the same four templates.

TemplateWhat it standardizes
Test planBackground, hypothesis, estimated business impact, primary and secondary metrics, sample size, audience, devices, and screenshots of each experience.
Tracking conventionsClick names that include the test ID and a unique UTM for every call to action, so every click ties to signups and CRM records.
Results readoutDate range and data caveats, results with significance, a full funnel for each experience, and numbered recommendations.
Process checklistPre-launch, post-launch and end-of-test tasks, from QA and heatmaps to launch emails, a CRM check and the archive.

Sharing what we learn

A testing program scales only when the rest of marketing brings the ideas, so I make the work visible and easy to join.

  • Brainstorm workshops that cover testing basics, a deep dive into a page’s data and how to write a strong hypothesis, then end with a prioritized list of ideas. I’ve run them for the AWS homepage and for Elastic’s security journey.
  • A monthly program review open to all of marketing: tests running, last month’s results and what’s next. It opens with a “Which test won?” vote, so people take part instead of multitasking.
  • Intro to A/B testing training on sample size, significance and minimum detectable effect, so partners bring stronger ideas.
  • Launch and results emails, Slack updates, a shared test calendar and a wiki, plus monthly and quarterly reviews with leadership.

How I lead

I lead people the way I approach visitors: by understanding them first.

  • Empathy first. I learn what each person needs before I ask for anything.
  • Psychological safety. Half-formed ideas and losing tests are welcome, because that’s where the learning is. I protect a sustainable pace so people can do their best work for the long run.
  • Coaching to your goals. Growth plans are built around each person’s career goals and passions, not just the team’s needs.
  • Authenticity. I say what I think, admit what I don’t know and give credit to the people who did the work.

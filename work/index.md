## Fewer, better trial signups: three tests that rebuilt the path to Elastic Cloud

The goal wasn’t just more signups. It was more people who start a trial, bring in their data and become customers. Three tests, each shaped by the one before, got us there, and the last one lifted trial signups 69%.

### The problem

The homepage’s main button said “Get started” without explaining what that meant. Behind it, the campaigns team had built a new trial landing page for nearly every campaign, more than 30 in all, then replaced them with one shared trial overview page. Designed by committee, it ended up generic. A visitor couldn’t tell how the trial worked, what to do during it, where to find help, or why it was worth their time.

### The hypothesis

If we set clear expectations about the trial at every step, more of the people who sign up would start a trial and use it.

1.  Test 1 · Homepage call to action · Jan–Feb 2022

    #### “Get started” or “Start free trial”?

    We changed the homepage hero button from “Get started” to “Start free trial” and added “Try Elastic free for 14 days. No credit card required.” Nearly 400,000 visitors saw the test.

    “Start free trial” drew 55% fewer clicks and 30% fewer total signups. But 21.8% of the people who clicked it finished signing up, versus 11.2% for “Get started,” a 95% lift, and data ingestion and paid conversions held even. Visitors who clicked a clear “Start free trial” knew what they were signing up for. It became the default call to action across elastic.co.

2.  Test 2 · A trial overview step · Apr–Sep 2022

    #### Add a step on purpose

    Instead of going straight to registration, the trial button went to the trial overview page first. We were adding friction, but we expected it to be healthy friction that prepared people for the trial. We ran it twice.

    On the homepage (April–May), total signups fell 45%, but 85.5% of the people who signed up started a trial, compared with 59% in the original. On the security, Elasticsearch and Kibana pages (May–September), the variant again showed a higher data ingestion rate, but other teams changed those pages’ main buttons mid-test, so we paused it rather than call a winner. Directional, not conclusive, and it pointed us to the overview page itself.

3.  Test 3 · Trial overview redesign · Feb–Apr 2023

    #### Make the step worth taking

    If visitors were going to pass through the overview page, it had to answer their questions. I recommended a redesign organized around them: how the trial works, what you get, what to do once you’re in, where to find resources, and why it matters, with customer stories as proof.

    Trial signups rose from 7.0% to 11.9% of page visitors (+69%) and trial starts rose 64%, both significant at p \< 0.001. In the weeks after launch, signups from the page stayed about 10% above where they were before the test.

**+95%**signup completion after a clearer call to action

**59% → 86%**of signups who started a trial after the overview step

**+69%**trial signups from the redesigned overview page

**+64%**trial starts from the same page

Not all friction is bad friction. Clear words and a useful step trade a little volume for people who are ready to use the product, so we judge trial work on starts, usage and conversion, not clicks alone.

## From a stalled redesign to a leadership team that said yes

Designing a better homepage was the easy part. The hard part was getting a passionate, opinionated leadership team to agree on one. The second time, we changed how we asked, and five of Elastic’s most important pages shipped a day ahead of the deadline.

### The problem

In 2023, Elastic paid a creative agency to redesign the whole website. It took a lot of time and money, and nobody loved the result, but it was too costly not to launch. Leadership lived with it, unhappily.

In 2025, we tried to rethink just the homepage with an in-house team. We spent two months presenting complete designs, and every round stalled on opinions. The project was shelved.

### What we changed

When we picked it up again, we kept the work in-house but changed the process. Instead of bringing leaders a finished page to react to, we brought them into every decision, one piece at a time, with a recommendation and the evidence behind it.

1.  Stage 1 · Research

    #### Start with evidence, not opinions

    The SEO team audited the homepage, platform page and three solutions pages against nine leading B2B software companies. A section became a requirement if at least seven of the nine competitors had it. Each page got a score, and none scored above 6 out of 10.

    The audit found headlines with no keywords, missing FAQ sections that AI assistants rely on, and a homepage that leaned so heavily on search that AI tools described Elastic mainly as a search company. It turned into a component checklist for each page, a shared problem, platform, proof and call-to-action structure for the solutions pages, and an internal linking map.

2.  Stage 2 · Content strategy, one section at a time

    #### Agree on the parts before the whole

    We turned the research into wireframes and walked leaders through the homepage section by section. For the hero, we showed three approaches (product-led, solution-led and persona-led), how competitors use each, what each would look like for Elastic, and our recommendation: lead with the product category. We repeated that for the platform, solutions, customer stories, news and even the footer.

3.  Stage 3 · Visual direction

    #### Three looks, one homepage

    A week later, we showed the homepage in three visual directions: a technical, developer-first look; a polished, enterprise look; and a hybrid of the two. Seeing the same agreed structure in each made the choice about style, not content, and leadership reached consensus.

4.  Stage 4 · Real content

    #### Show the finished page

    A week after that, we brought the final design with real content in the agreed structure. Approval followed, and we built and launched all five pages one day ahead of the deadline.

### Planning to a hard deadline

The project ran from one shared plan with numbered requirements that every ticket referenced, user stories from each team, and an explicit out-of-scope list that moved pricing, trial and download pages to a second wave. Its audiences included AI agents, language models and crawlers alongside developers and buyers. Design moved through dated versions, from an early concept in mid-July to engineering-ready files in mid-August, so the security page could launch for Black Hat and the rest for ElasticON.

### Keeping it from drifting

The old homepage had decayed because every product launch rewrote the hero. So the redesign came with a page update protocol. It splits each page into zones (an evergreen core, a proof layer, a capability grid, a “what’s new” module, an FAQ block and internal links), each with an owner and an update schedule. A decision guide says which zones each kind of launch is allowed to touch. New products update the capability grid and FAQ; events only ever go in the announcement bar.

### The results

Within a couple of weeks of launch, bounce rate dropped, and time on site, scroll depth and click-through on every solutions page all improved.

**\<9% → ~25%**homepage visitors reaching the bottom of the page

**~20% → ~60%**visitors who saw solutions content

**\<1% → ~4%**click-through on the security solution

Leaders don’t align on a finished page; they align on decisions. Break the work into choices, show the options and the evidence, make a recommendation, and the finished design becomes a formality.

## Product pages that still shape aws.amazon.com ten years later

I was hired at AWS to own one problem: the product detail pages. In four months we built a new structure, information architecture and content strategy for them. A decade later, the visual style has changed but that structure is still the one AWS uses.

### The problem

Product marketing managers built their own pages in Adobe Experience Manager from a library of reusable components. There was no template, no shared information architecture and no content strategy, so each manager added their own flavor. With 75 products on the site, it looked like it had been designed by 75 different people.

### Why we didn’t start with A/B tests

A test needs a control, and there was no single default experience to test against. So instead of testing our way to an answer, we researched our way to one, using several kinds of data that each answered a different question.

1.  Step 1 · Web analytics

    #### What visitors were doing

    Traffic sources, what people clicked and ignored, time on site and page depth across the product pages, along with an audit of 500+ pages. It showed what was and wasn’t working, but not why.

2.  Step 2 · Card sort

    #### How visitors expect content to be organized

    In a card sort, participants get a stack of cards, each naming a piece of content, and group them into the categories that make sense to them. Ours asked where people would put things like case studies, pricing details and documentation under headings such as features, pricing, resources and FAQ. It showed us visitors’ mental model of a product page, including content they didn’t expect to find at all.

3.  Step 3 · Moderated usability sessions

    #### Watching people hunt for answers

    Think of it as a treasure hunt. While we watched their screens, participants tried tasks like “Find the pricing for Amazon S3.” We could see exactly where navigation broke down.

    At least 25% of participants left the site at some point and searched Google instead, because that was easier than finding the answer on our pages.

4.  Step 4 · Focus groups in Houston, Chicago and New York

    #### What people need, and who they trust

    Over about six weeks, we ran groups across industries, with both decision-makers and developers, and with people who knew AWS and people who didn’t. We asked where they look for information when solving a problem and whose recommendations they trust.

### What we built

With a UX designer and a visual designer, I turned the research into a set of five templates for every product, so each page has one job:

- **Overview:** what the product is, what it delivers and how it helps, with customer proof.
- **Features:** capabilities and what you can accomplish with them.
- **Pricing:** how the product is priced.
- **Resources:** documentation, webinars, videos, virtual events and community meetups.
- **FAQ:** answers to every common question about the product.

We validated the templates with real AWS customers at the AWS Summits in San Francisco and Chicago and refined the designs from their feedback.

### Getting it live

Engineering built the new components the templates needed. I built the templates in AEM myself, then created a training program so product marketing managers could copy a template, fill it in for their product and publish it. Because I was faster at it, I also migrated a large share of the pages by hand; one teammate and I moved more than 800 pages into the new templates.

**4 months**from research to a new page strategy

**5**templates per product, each with one job

**800+**pages migrated by hand, by me and one teammate

**10 years**and the structure is still in use

When there’s no baseline to test against, research is the fastest path. Each method answered a different question, and together they gave us a structure durable enough to outlast a decade of redesigns.

## Four losses and a lift: five tests on Ancestry’s search results

Sometimes the most useful result is learning that you’re solving the wrong problem. We spent four tests trying to get visitors to give us more information. The fifth stopped asking and changed what we showed them, and that’s what lifted signups.

### The problem

The search results page was one of Ancestry’s top five entry pages for new visitors, with about 31,000 arriving there each day. Fewer than 1% of new visitors converted. Analytics, heatmaps and session recordings showed that most people searched with only a first and last name, sometimes a birth or death year, and only about 10% used the tools to refine their search.

More details mean more accurate results. Better results make a visitor more likely to click a record, and since records open only with a subscription or trial, that click is the path to signup.

### The first hypothesis

If we asked new visitors for more details about the person they were looking for, they would find the right records faster and more of them would subscribe.

1.  Test 1 · A guidance banner · Jun–Jul 2015

    #### Ask above the results

    On desktop and tablet, a banner above the search results asked visitors for more details about their ancestor.

    Signup conversion fell 10%. But the people who did subscribe were better customers: gross subscription adds rose 16%, cancellations fell 30% and lifetime revenue per visitor rose 14%. Session recordings showed fewer than 10% of visitors used the banner, and many scrolled past without seeing it.

2.  Test 2 · A sticky guidance panel · Aug–Sep 2015

    #### Keep the question in view

    Since visitors scrolled past the banner, we moved the guidance into a panel that stayed on screen as they scrolled through results.

    The original won. Signup conversion was 2.6% lower and lifetime revenue per visitor 3.8% lower, and recordings showed fewer than 20% of visitors interacted with the panel.

3.  Test 3 · Choose your own adventure · 2016

    #### Offer a different way to start

    Maybe searching records wasn’t the right first step for everyone. A banner offered two ways to begin, searching records or building a family tree, tested at the top of the results, at the bottom, and at the bottom without page numbers.

    Flat to slightly down across about 737,000 visitors per version. The bottom placement without page numbers cut signups 5.2%.

4.  Test 4 · Results styling · May–Jun 2016

    #### Match how people scan

    About 70% of visitors arrived from search engines, which train people to scan the left side of a results page. We moved each person’s name from the right side of a result to the left.

    No change in record views or signups.

5.  Test 5 · Record thumbnails · Jun–Aug 2016

    #### Show the records, don’t ask for more

    Instead of asking visitors to do more, we changed what they saw: each search result included a thumbnail image of the actual record.

    Signups rose 3%, gross subscription adds 2% and lifetime revenue 2%. Visitors left the test after signing up and saw the old design afterward, which may have held down billthrough, so we planned to fix that in the next version.

**+3%**signups from record thumbnails

**+2%**gross subscription adds

**+2%**lifetime revenue

### What we learned

Most visitors probably didn’t know much more about their ancestor than a name and a couple of dates; if they had, they would have entered it the first time. No placement could ask for information people didn’t have. Showing them what the records looked like did what asking couldn’t.

When visitors skip a form field, first ask whether they have the answer. If they don’t, stop asking and change what you show them.

## Turning an hour of ticket triage into the push of a button

Two systems that didn’t talk to each other were costing the web team up to an hour on every request. A Claude skill I built now does the work in seconds, and every requester learns right away what we still need from them.

### The problem

Marketing requested work in Adobe Workfront. Engineering, including the web team, tracked work in GitHub. Workfront was far more complicated than the web team needed, and we wanted to own our intake in GitHub.

But Elastic didn’t have enough GitHub seats for everyone in marketing, and Workfront’s built-in connector to GitHub proved hard to set up. So every day, someone on the team read each new Workfront ticket, worked out what it was asking for and what was missing, and copied it into GitHub by hand. Each ticket took 30 minutes to an hour.

### What I built

I used Claude Code to write a skill that reads everything a requester entered in a Workfront ticket, checks it against what we need to scope the work, and takes two actions:

1.  Action 1 · A GitHub issue, ready to work

    #### Created and labeled automatically

    The skill opens an issue in the web team’s repository and labels it by the work involved: whether it needs UX or SEO, and whether it’s a content update to an existing page or a new or redesigned experience.

2.  Action 2 · A reply to the requester

    #### A checklist of what’s missing

    Based on the gaps it finds, the skill drafts an email to the person who submitted the ticket, with a checklist of the details we need before we can size and scope the work.

### Fixing the front door too

Alongside the tool, we consolidated 16 request templates into 6 and partnered with Marketing Operations on an automated Workfront-to-GitHub sync. I also wrote two things for requesters:

- **A project request brief** that asks for the problem, why now, the success metric, the audience and the scope signals that set an effort tier from extra small to extra large. Large projects get a program manager and a requirements doc and go to leadership for prioritization. Incomplete briefs get five business days before the ticket closes.
- **A guide to justifying a request** for people new to web work or analytics. It turns vague answers (“developers,” “raise awareness”) into specific ones and lists where to find the evidence, from Search Console and analytics to asking an AI assistant how it describes Elastic today.

With better briefs, the team could estimate effort accurately and leaders could stack-rank projects on real trade-offs.

**30–60 min → seconds**to triage each request

**16 → 6**request templates

**~90%**of the automated sync complete

The fastest way to speed up a team is to fix what happens before the work starts. When a process is manual and repetitive, it’s a candidate for AI, and the people closest to the work are the right ones to build it.
